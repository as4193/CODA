\begin{abstract}
Anomaly detectors for cyber-physical systems are calibrated at full precision and then quantized to run on microcontrollers. Quantization shifts the anomaly-score distribution while largely preserving its ranking, so a conformal threshold inherited from the full-precision model can silently lose its false-alarm guarantee. We propose CODA (Calibrate Once, Deploy Anywhere), which calibrates the threshold once on a host GPU and gives each device a label-free correction measured from how its integer arithmetic perturbs the score. We will test whether this keeps the realized false-alarm rate within a target bound on three microcontroller families (STM32, ESP32, RP2350) using public industrial anomaly-detection benchmarks, and report latency and memory overhead on each board.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
conformal prediction, anomaly detection, quantization, TinyML, cyber-physical systems

\section{Problem Statement and Motivation}
Industrial sensors increasingly detect faults on the device itself, which cuts latency and keeps raw data local. To fit in kilobytes of SRAM, models are converted to 8-bit integers (INT8) after training~\cite{jacob2018,david2021,lin2020}. Operators also need a predictable false-alarm rate. Conformal anomaly detection provides one: a threshold that bounds false alarms on normal data at a chosen level $\alpha$~\cite{bates2023,angelopoulos2023}. That guarantee holds only for the model that was calibrated.

In practice, the threshold is set once at full precision and copied to every device. Each device runs a different integer model, with its own kernels and rounding, so its scores drift and the guarantee is lost. Recalibrating on every device is often impractical: calibration data may be too large for flash, too sensitive to ship, or too costly to collect for each hardware variant in a fleet.

\textbf{Research question:} Can a threshold calibrated once at full precision, plus a label-free per-device shift correction, keep the realized false-alarm rate of INT8 anomaly detectors on heterogeneous microcontrollers within a target bound?\\
\textbf{Hypothesis:} On all three boards, the corrected threshold keeps the false-alarm rate on normal test data within $\alpha+\delta$ (with $\delta=0.01$, for $\alpha\in\{0.01,0.05\}$), while the inherited threshold exceeds it, and detection recall stays within 5 percentage points of per-device recalibration.

\section{Related Work and Research Gap}
Table~\ref{tab:rw} summarizes the closest work. A NeurIPS 2026 workshop study showed that 4-bit quantization of robot-policy failure detectors leaves AUC almost unchanged while the coverage of an inherited conformal threshold drops from 0.77 to 0.41 (target 0.90), because quantization roughly translates the score distribution~\cite{vla2026}; its repair is empirical and its quantization simulated. A 2026 benchmark measured conformal prediction on compressed LLMs, but recalibrates every compressed model~\cite{compress2026}. A retrieval study certifies when quantized outputs match full-precision ones, not false-alarm rates~\cite{retrieval2026}. On microcontrollers, TCUQ and HDUQ-HAR run conformal calibration on the deployed model directly~\cite{tcuq2025,hduq2026}. Meanwhile, INT8 kernel paths have been shown to diverge layer by layer~\cite{alibi2026}, so each chip may need its own correction. Conformal theory for non-exchangeable data exists~\cite{barber2023,gibbs2021} but has not been applied to this setting.

\textbf{Research gap.} No existing method restores a conformal false-alarm guarantee for a full-precision threshold deployed on integer hardware, with proof, on real microcontrollers, for streaming sensor data.

\section{Proposed Method}
\subsection{Problem Formulation}
Let $x$ be a sensor window, $s(x)$ the full-precision anomaly score, and $s_d(x)$ the score of the INT8 model on device $d$. Split-conformal calibration on $n$ normal windows sets $\tau$ as the $\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$-th smallest calibration score~\cite{bates2023}. With score shift $\Delta_d(x)=s_d(x)-s(x)$, we set
\begin{equation}
\tau_d=\tau+\hat q_d,
\end{equation}
where $\hat q_d$ is the $\lceil (m+1)(1-\delta)\rceil$-th smallest of $\Delta_d$ over $m$ unlabeled probe windows. A union bound gives a false-alarm rate on device $d$ of at most $\alpha+\delta$ when probe and test windows are exchangeable; we will prove this formally and extend it to temporally dependent data using block calibration~\cite{barber2023}.

\subsection{Approach}
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the pipeline. (1) Windows of length 64 (stride 1) are z-normalized with training statistics. (2) A 1D-convolutional autoencoder (three conv layers, under 100\,KB in INT8) is trained on normal windows; the score is reconstruction mean-squared error. The last 20\% of normal training data, split in time, is the calibration set. (3) Each board's model is produced by TensorFlow Lite full-integer post-training quantization with 500 representative windows, and compiled with TFLite Micro using CMSIS-NN (STM32, RP2350) or ESP-NN (ESP32)~\cite{david2021}. (4) $m=500$ probe windows are streamed to each board over UART; on-device scores give $\Delta_d$. (5) $\tau_d$ is flashed to the board. No calibration data leaves the host.

\section{Evaluation Plan}
\subsection{Datasets and Baselines}
\textbf{Data:} Server Machine Dataset (SMD)~\cite{su2019} as the primary benchmark; SWaT (water treatment, by request) as the industrial case; NASA SMAP/MSL as a backup. Because common anomaly benchmarks have known flaws~\cite{wu2023}, the main metric is false-alarm rate on normal data, where labels are least disputed. \textbf{Hardware:} STM32 Nucleo (Cortex-M4/M7), ESP32-S3 (Xtensa LX7), and Raspberry Pi Pico~2 (RP2350, Cortex-M33), under 100~USD in total, plus existing lab GPUs. \textbf{Baselines:} (B1) inherited threshold $\tau$; (B2) per-device recalibration with the full calibration set (upper bound); (B3) on-device streaming quantile recalibration, in the spirit of TCUQ and adaptive conformal inference~\cite{tcuq2025,gibbs2021}; (B4) the label-free realignment repair of~\cite{vla2026}.

\subsection{Metrics}
Task metrics: realized false-alarm rate versus target $\alpha\in\{0.01,0.05,0.1\}$, detection recall, and AUC. Systems measures: inference latency per window (on-chip cycle counter), peak SRAM, and flash, including the overhead of the correction. All results are averaged over 5 seeds (training and calibration split), with 95\% confidence intervals.
