# CODA: Related Work (draft for Progress Report 1)

Draft of Oct 29, 2026. Citation keys refer to `docs/refs.bib`. Every entry there was checked against its arXiv, publisher, proceedings, or Crossref page on Oct 29, 2026, and the URL used is written above each BibTeX entry.

## 1. Conformal prediction and conformal anomaly detection

Conformal prediction turns any score into a decision rule with a finite-sample error guarantee, assuming only that calibration and test points are exchangeable [vovk2022; angelopoulos2023]. For anomaly detection the relevant form is the conformal p-value: a test window is compared with the scores of held-out normal windows, and flagging p-values below alpha bounds the false-alarm rate by alpha. Bates et al. [bates2023] showed that these p-values are marginally valid but dependent, and gave a conditional calibration step that makes them usable with multiple-testing procedures. Marandon et al. [marandon2024] extended this to false discovery rate control when the score itself is learned from part of the data. On the applied side, Laxhammar and Falkman [laxhammar2015] used inductive (split) conformal anomaly detection on vessel trajectories, Ishimtsev et al. [ishimtsev2017] applied it to univariate streams, and Rheey and Park [rheey2026] recently used a split-conformal threshold on autoencoder reconstruction errors to hold the false-alarm rate of a network intrusion detector within budget. All of these works compute the threshold with the same model that later scores the test data. None asks what happens when the scoring function itself changes between calibration and deployment, which is the situation created by quantization.

## 2. Conformal inference under dependence and distribution shift

Sensor windows are neither independent nor stationary, so the exchangeability assumption needs care. Barber et al. [barber2023] bound the coverage loss of weighted conformal methods by the total-variation distance between the true data and its swapped versions, which gives a clean way to state how much a guarantee degrades. Weighted conformal prediction [tibshirani2019] repairs coverage under covariate shift when the likelihood ratio is known or estimated. For time series, Chernozhukov et al. [chernozhukov2018] use block permutations, Xu and Xie [xu2021] give approximate coverage for strongly mixing errors with EnbPI, and Oliveira et al. [oliveira2024] show that plain split conformal stays nearly valid for beta-mixing data, with a penalty that shrinks as the calibration set grows. A second line adjusts the threshold online: adaptive conformal inference [gibbs2021] and its aggregated variant AgACI [zaffran2022] track a target long-run miscoverage under arbitrary shift, and Martinez Gil et al. [martinezgil2026] apply weighted, adaptive quantiles to anomaly scores from time-series foundation models. Conformal risk control [angelopoulos2024crc] generalizes the guarantee from coverage to any bounded monotone loss. These tools treat shift in the input distribution. In CODA the inputs do not change; the score function does, so the useful pieces are the mixing-based validity results (for block calibration) and the idea of paying a stated price, here delta, for a known source of mismatch.

## 3. Calibration transfer and recalibration across models

The closest methodological neighbor is Conformal Calibration Transfer [doula2026], which moves labeled calibration from a source space to a target space through unlabeled paired observations and then corrects the remaining mismatch with unlabeled target inputs, with finite-sample target coverage. CODA's probe windows are paired observations in exactly this sense (the same window scored by the host model and by the device), but in CODA the pairing is between two versions of one model rather than two sensing modalities, and the correction is a single scalar quantile that a microcontroller can store. Label-free recalibration under input shift has also been studied through test-time quantile estimation [yilmaz2022] and online adjustment from unlabeled data [kasa2025]. For compressed models, Tong et al. [tong2026] benchmark conformal prediction on quantized and pruned LLMs but recalibrate each compressed model, Zhou et al. [zhou2026] certify with split conformal that quantized and full-precision retrieval outputs agree, and Salem et al. [salem2026] recommend recalibrating at deployed precision. El Allam and Hamlich [elallam2026] run Mondrian conformal prediction for ECG classification on an ESP32-S3 and compute the thresholds on the INT8 model's own outputs. The shared answer in this group is "recalibrate on the deployed model with in-domain data". That is a strong baseline (our B2m result agrees), but it needs representative private data at or near every device.

## 4. What quantization does to model outputs beyond accuracy

Accuracy is a weak proxy for whether a quantized model behaves like its parent. Hooker et al. [hooker2019] found that compressed networks with matched top-line accuracy lose disproportionately on a small set of examples, and Xia et al. [xia2021] reported that better calibrated full-precision networks can degrade more after post-training quantization. For thresholds specifically, Bysani [bysani2026] showed that 4-bit quantization of robot-policy failure detectors keeps AUC almost unchanged while an inherited conformal threshold drops from 0.77 to 0.41 coverage, because the score distribution is roughly translated; the repair is empirical and the quantization simulated. Duan [duan2026] reports the same staleness for activation monitors after LLM updates and repairs it with label-free realignment. The numerics also differ across software stacks running the same nominal model. Across 75 platforms, Schlögl et al. [schlogl2023] found that a fixed CNN with fixed inputs gives different outputs, mainly because of SIMD use on CPUs and runtime choice of convolution algorithm on GPUs; quantization reduced but did not remove these deviations. Chen [chen2026alibi] localized divergence between INT8 kernel paths to most layers of an LLM. Our own finding that ESP-DL and ONNX Runtime are not bit-identical to TFLite, with ESP-DL shifting scores about eight times more, fits this picture. What is missing is a link from these measured deviations to a decision-level guarantee.

## 5. Quantization methods and TinyML deployment stacks

Integer-only inference with an affine mapping and int32 accumulation [jacob2018] is the scheme used by TensorFlow Lite Micro [david2021] and its CMSIS-NN kernels for Cortex-M [lai2018]. The two practitioner white papers [krishnamoorthi2018; nagel2021] document the design choices (per-tensor versus per-channel scales, symmetric versus asymmetric ranges, calibration of activation ranges) that differ between toolchains and change outputs. At 4 bits, post-training quantization needs more than rounding to nearest: AdaRound [nagel2020] learns the rounding direction and BRECQ [li2021brecq] reconstructs block outputs. Bias correction [nagel2019] is worth noting because it removes the expected shift in layer outputs caused by quantization, which is an activation-level cousin of CODA's score-level correction, but it carries no guarantee on the final decision. On the systems side, MCUNet [lin2020] co-designs architecture and inference engine for microcontrollers, and MLPerf Tiny [banbury2021] includes an autoencoder anomaly-detection task, which shows that this model class is a standard MCU workload. Vendor stacks follow their own conventions: ESP-DL [espdl] uses the ESP-PPQ quantizer [espppq], a fork of PPQ with chip-specific rules; ST Edge AI Core [stedgeai] imports TFLite and ONNX QDQ models; and ONNX Runtime [ortquant] documents a saturation issue for one INT8 format on some x86 instruction sets only. So "the same INT8 model" can mean different integer arithmetic on each target.

## 6. On-device anomaly detection and TinyML uncertainty

Uncertainty estimation on microcontrollers has moved toward single-pass monitors: QUTE [ghanathe2024] uses early-exit-assisted ensembles, SNAP-UQ [lamaakal2026snapuq] predicts the next activation with small INT8 heads, TCUQ [lamaakal2025tcuq] adds streaming conformal calibration of a temporal-consistency score, and HDUQ-HAR [lamaakal2026hduq] runs conformal prediction with hyperdimensional computing on an MCU. In every case the calibration is done on the deployed model, so no full-precision threshold is transferred. For evaluation, the Server Machine Dataset [su2019], SMAP and MSL [hundman2018], and SWaT [goh2017] are the usual multivariate benchmarks, but Wu and Keogh [wu2023] document mislabeled anomalies, trivial solvability, and run-to-failure bias in several of them. This supports our choice to make the false-alarm rate on normal data the primary metric, since it depends least on anomaly labels.

## 7. Fleets, federation, and privacy

When data are spread across clients, federated conformal methods combine local quantiles into a global threshold with coverage guarantees, either with partial exchangeability [lu2023] or in one communication round using a quantile of quantiles, with a locally private variant [humbert2023]. Private prediction sets [angelopoulos2022private] add differential privacy to the calibration step itself. These methods assume the clients share one scoring model and differ in data; CODA has the reverse structure, with shared data distribution and per-device scoring arithmetic. On-device adaptation, such as online learning of an anomaly autoencoder on an MCU [ren2021] or training under 256 KB [lin2022], is the other way to handle device differences, but it needs in-domain data on the device and offers no false-alarm guarantee.

## Gap

Taken together, the literature gives (a) false-alarm guarantees for a fixed scoring model, also under mixing and shift (Sections 1 and 2); (b) evidence that quantization and different integer kernels translate or perturb scores while keeping ranking (Section 4); and (c) recalibration recipes that restore validity by recomputing the threshold on the deployed model with in-domain data (Sections 3, 6, and 7). No published method keeps a host-calibrated conformal false-alarm guarantee when the threshold is moved to integer hardware whose numerics differ by device or toolchain, without bringing labeled or in-domain data to the device. CODA fills this gap with a two-stage bound: the host threshold controls false alarms at alpha, and a per-device correction, the (1 minus delta) conformal quantile of the score difference on m paired probe windows, adds at most delta. Its specific contributions relative to prior work are: a finite-sample bound of alpha plus delta that holds for each device and toolchain separately; a correction that needs only unlabeled probes, which may be public or synthetic, so no private calibration data is placed on the device; a block-calibration variant for dependent sensor streams; and measurements on real microcontrollers with several vendor toolchains rather than simulated quantization. The honest boundary is that when in-domain normal probes are available, recalibrating directly on those probes is as good or better; CODA's claim is restricted to the public-probe and small-m regime, and experiment E3 has to show that score differences measured on public probes transfer to in-domain data.
